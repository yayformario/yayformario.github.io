## All subsets vs all contiguous subsets
All subsets is n<sup>2</sup>, and we know that contiguous and non contiguous are subsets. So the upper bound is n<sup>2</sup>. 
![contiguous](/media/posts/3/contiguous.png)


## Geometric Visualization
Let's begin by manually calculating all the subarrays by hand on a small example. The formula claims we should have 10 unique subarrays for an array size of n = 4. We can verify that by drawing them all out. 
![contiguous](/media/posts/3/example.png)

Without the numbers, this can also be drawn as the following
![alt text](/media/posts/3/image.png)

Now let's circle back and draw the full n<sup>2</sup> grid. 
![alt text](/media/posts/3/grid.png)

The area of an nxn grid is n<sup>2</sup>. The diagonal of an nxn grid will always be n. So let's get rid of the diagonal to get n<sup>2</sup> - n.

This leaves two perfect triangle. One with our data and one without our data. This means that the triangle with the data we want is (n<sup>2</sup> - n) / 2
![alt text](/media/posts/3/diagonal.png)

Lastly, let's add be the diagonal (which was size n) back to the triangle we just calculated. This gets us  (n (n+1) ) / 2.
![alt text](/media/posts/3/trianglediag.png)
